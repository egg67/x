FRAGMENT I

Pompey the Great was born in 106 BCE into a family whose influence within the Republic would provide the foundation for his later rise. By the end of his life, he would be regarded as one of Rome's greatest commanders.

FRAGMENT II

The birth of Julius Caesar is traditionally placed in 100 BCE. Although born into an ancient patrician family, his early position offered little indication of the political dominance he would later achieve.

FRAGMENT III

In 70 BCE, Pompey and Crassus held the consulship together. Their election marked a significant shift in the political balance of the Republic and increased Pompey's influence in Rome.

FRAGMENT IV

The political alliance later known as the First Triumvirate emerged around 60 BCE, bringing Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus into a partnership intended to advance their respective ambitions.

FRAGMENT V

Caesar's consulship in 59 BCE strengthened his political position and secured the commands that would eventually lead to his conquest of Gaul.

FRAGMENT VI

The crossing of the Rubicon in 49 BCE transformed a political dispute into open civil war. Caesar's advance forced many of his rivals, including Pompey, to abandon Rome.

FRAGMENT VII

After his defeat at Pharsalus, Pompey fled eastward. In 48 BCE, he arrived in Egypt, where he was assassinated shortly after reaching the shore.

ARCHIVIST'S COMMENTARY

Reference: REG-VII · MS 1847.3

The present register preserves only seven entries considered sufficiently complete for study. Earlier copies refer to additional material, though no reliable versions are known to survive.

Comparison with surviving references suggests that the register was assembled from several damaged accounts rather than copied from a single original source. The compiler appears to have combined fragmented material into a unified record before preservation.

Although the surviving fragments likely originated from different documents, the final arrangement displays a deliberate consistency. The sequence preserved here appears more systematic than would be expected from the condition of the source material itself.

Certain annotations employ ordinal conventions commonly encountered in Roman administrative records. Such notation would have been familiar to educated officials and therefore rarely required explanation within contemporary documents.
